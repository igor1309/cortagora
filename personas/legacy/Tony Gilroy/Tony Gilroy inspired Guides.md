# Learning From Tony Gilroy: The Aspiring Creator’s Guide

## 1. **Obsess Over Character**
- **Everything starts with character.** Gilroy’s worlds are compelling because every person has goals, secrets, needs, and contradictions.
- **Exercise:** For every major character, write a “private memo” (never shown to the audience) answering:  
  - What do they want?  
  - What are they afraid of?  
  - What are they hiding?  
  - What will they *never* do?

## 2. **Design Systems, Not Just Stories**
- Gilroy’s drama often comes from *systems* (law firms, agencies, the Empire) rather than single “villains.”  
- **Think:** What’s the “machine” your characters are trapped in? What rules do they have to play by?
- **Exercise:** Diagram the system (organization, bureaucracy, family, etc.) and map where each character stands in it.

## 3. **Master Subtext**
- **Never say it all.** Tension rises when what’s said isn’t what’s meant.
- **Gilroy’s tip:** Write the “surface” dialogue, then re-write so real intentions are buried, letting the audience work for it.
- **Exercise:** Take a scene and rewrite it three times, each with less direct exposition.

## 4. **Cut Ruthlessly**
- Gilroy’s scripts are *lean*—if a scene doesn’t serve the story or reveal character, it goes.
- **Exercise:** After your first draft, challenge yourself to remove 10–20% of scenes or lines. If it hurts, you’re doing it right.

## 5. **Write in Layers**
- Every scene should operate on at least three levels: what’s literally happening, the emotional stakes, and the unspoken/subtextual stakes.
- **Exercise:** Annotate your script’s scenes with these three layers. Are any scenes flat? Rework them.

## 6. **Trust the Audience**
- Gilroy expects viewers to keep up—so should you. Resist spelling everything out.
- **Exercise:** Remove one piece of exposition per draft. Does the scene still make sense? If so, leave it out.

## 7. **Embrace Moral Ambiguity**
- Characters are most compelling when their choices are messy and difficult, not clean or obvious.
- **Exercise:** Force your protagonist into a lose-lose decision; what do they choose, and what does it cost?

## 8. **Let Theme Emerge from Story**
- Don’t start with a “message.” Start with situation, system, and character; let the theme emerge.
- **Exercise:** After a draft, ask yourself what themes *emerged*—don’t try to inject them artificially.

## 9. **Focus on Scene Craft**
- Each scene should be a little drama of its own. Avoid static or “functional” scenes.
- **Exercise:** For each scene, ask: If I only had this scene to show my skills, would someone want to see more?

## 10. **Study Real-World Structures**
- Research how real organizations, bureaucracies, and systems work. The authenticity will elevate your world-building.
- **Exercise:** Read a book or interview about a real institution (spy agency, corporation, political campaign) and steal details.

- **Record and re-read real conversations**: Notice how people rarely say what they mean; bring that to your writing.
- **Find feedback partners** who value subtlety and complexity, not just “cool plot twists.”

## Gilroy-inspired guide to employing AI as your script development partner

Here’s an advanced, **Gilroy-inspired guide to employing AI as your script development partner**—with *practical, creative, and critical* prompts and workflow suggestions.

# **Employing AI Assistants for Script Development**
*(A Gilroy-Influenced, Creator-Level Guide)*

## **1. Treat the AI as a Room of Writers, Not Just a Typing Tool**
- Think of your AI as your writer’s room—*challenge* it, *argue* with it, force it to defend its ideas.
- **Prompt:**  
  *“You are my co-writer. Give me three radically different versions of this scene, focusing on subtext and moral ambiguity.”*

## **2. Generate, Critique, and Iterate**
- Don’t just accept the AI’s first output. Ask for critique, then improvement.
- **Prompt:**  
  *“What’s missing from this dialogue? Where is it too on-the-nose? Suggest three cuts.”*

## **3. Build Character Depth**
- Use AI to flesh out character bios, contradictions, and off-screen motivations—then challenge those details.
- **Prompt:**  
  *“Give me a secret this character is hiding that would change the meaning of all their previous lines.”*

## **4. Emphasize Systems and World-Building**
- AI excels at generating plausible institutions, rules, or background systems. Ask it to diagram the power structure, or to invent a bureaucracy for your world.
- **Prompt:**  
  *“Describe the hierarchy and hidden rivalries inside the [organization] in three paragraphs of in-world prose.”*

## **5. Force Subtext and Layering**
- AI can quickly draft “surface” dialogue—but ask it to rework with more subtext, less exposition, and more ambiguity.
- **Prompt:**  
  *“Rewrite this conversation so both characters are hiding something—and their real intentions are only clear in subtext.”*

## **6. Cut Ruthlessly—With AI’s Help**
- Use AI to identify redundancy and bloat. Make it “ruthlessly edit” as Gilroy would.
- **Prompt:**  
  *“Which scenes could be removed without breaking character or story logic? Defend your answer.”*

## **7. Scene Analysis: The Three Levels**
- Have the AI analyze your scenes for: (a) surface action, (b) emotional arc, (c) subtext/theme.
- **Prompt:**  
  *“Break down this scene on three levels: surface action, emotional content, subtext. What’s missing?”*

## **8. Simulate the Audience’s Experience**
- Ask the AI to “be” your audience—what are they inferring? What is left unsaid? Where do they get ahead or fall behind?
- **Prompt:**  
  *“As a viewer, what questions am I left with after this scene? Where am I likely confused or bored?”*

## **9. Study and Deconstruct**
- Use AI to dissect Gilroy’s scenes (or your favorite scripts)—reverse-engineer their structure, stakes, and layers.
- **Prompt:**  
  *“Dissect the ‘Nobody’s Listening!’ speech from Andor. What makes it effective in terms of structure and subtext?”*

## **10. Theme Tracking and Emergence**
- Ask AI to read your draft and report back what themes emerge naturally (not just what you intended).
- **Prompt:**  
  *“What recurring motifs and themes can you find in this draft? Where are they most visible, and where do they feel forced?”*

---

## **Tips for Workflow**

- **Draft in Rounds:**  
  Generate, critique, revise, repeat. Use AI for radical rewrites, not just polishing.
- **Context is Key:**  
  Feed the AI as much context (scene goals, character needs, previous events) as possible for best results.
- **Avoid AI Clichés:**  
  Always ask AI for “originality,” “non-cliché choices,” or “unpredictable reversals.”

---

## **Sample Creative Session (Mini-Playbook)**

1. **Seed:**  
   “Scene: Rebel operative is caught. Authority figure interrogates. Both have secrets.”
2. **Draft:**  
   “AI, give me three versions: a) dialogue heavy, b) almost silent, c) told only via small talk.”
3. **Critique:**  
   “Which feels most Gilroy-esque and why? What would he cut?”
4. **Layer:**  
   “Rewrite version (b) so both are hiding personal motives as well as plot info.”
5. **Audience:**  
   “As an audience, what questions would I be asking after this scene?”

---

## **Golden Rule: Make the AI Work For You**
- The more you *push* your AI assistant—interrogating, critiquing, demanding layers and depth—the more “Gilroy” your scripts will become.
- Don’t let the AI default to “safe” or generic; *always* press for the uncomfortable, the ambiguous, the truly character-driven.
